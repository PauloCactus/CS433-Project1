\title{Application of Machine Learning in Predictive Coronary Heart Disease (MICHD) Diagnosis}

\author{Paul Barbereau, Amandine Dejardin, Samuel Perry\\
\textit{School of Computer and Communication Sciences, EPFL, Lausanne, Switzerland}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a leading cause of mortality worldwide, making early risk screening from accessible clinical and lifestyle indicators an important public health objective. In this work, we develop a complete machine learning pipeline to predict myocardial infarction or coronary heart disease (MICHD) from the 2015 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) dataset ($328{,}135$ training and $109{,}379$ test individuals across $321$ survey variables). To address severe missingness, heterogeneous survey coding, feature redundancy, and strong class imbalance ($8.83\%$ positive rate), we combine domain-informed selection of $47$ clinically relevant features with codebook-guided recoding, fold-wise median imputation, standardization, and $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression with decision threshold tuning. Evaluated via stratified-free 5-fold cross-validation, our approach achieves an F1 score of $0.4199 \pm 0.0030$ and an accuracy of $87.15\% \pm 0.05\%$, substantially outperforming uncleaned baselines and linear regression models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including myocardial infarction and coronary heart disease (MICHD), represent one of the primary causes of death globally. Early identification of high-risk individuals using non-invasive lifestyle, demographic, and clinical history questionnaires can support timely preventive interventions. In this project, we predict binary MICHD diagnosis ($y \in \{-1, 1\}$) using survey data from the CDC's 2015 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)~\cite{cdc_brfss_codebook_2015}.

Applying standard machine learning algorithms directly to the raw BRFSS dataset presents four key challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High dimensionality and irrelevant variables:} The raw dataset contains $D = 321$ columns, many of which are administrative metadata (e.g., state codes, interview dates, phone line weights) unrelated to pathology.
    \item \textbf{Survey-specific coding of missing and zero values:} Rather than standard \texttt{NaN} entries, BRFSS encodes ``Don't know/Not sure,'' ``Refused,'' and ``None/Zero'' using variable-specific multi-digit integers (such as $7, 9, 77, 88, 99, 777, 888, 9999$), which severely distort numerical distributions if treated as continuous quantities.
    \item \textbf{Multicollinearity:} Several columns are deterministic transformations of others (e.g., continuous age, 5-year age buckets, and $>65$ indicators), leading to rank-deficient design matrices.
    \item \textbf{Severe class imbalance:} Only $28{,}975$ of the $328{,}135$ training respondents ($8.83\%$) report MICHD, causing standard $\tau = 0.50$ probability thresholds to favor the majority negative class and miss most positive cases.
\end{enumerate}
To overcome these obstacles without external libraries, we implement six foundational linear and logistic models in \texttt{implementations.py} and pair our best classifier ($\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression) with codebook-driven preprocessing and 5-fold cross-validation in \texttt{run.py}.

\section{Models and Methodology}

\subsection{Model Selection and Baseline Implementations}
We first implemented the six core optimization and regression methods seen in lectures in \texttt{implementations.py}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linear models (MSE loss):} \texttt{least\_squares} (normal equations $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{y}$), \texttt{ridge\_regression} (adding $2N\lambda\mathbf{I}$ to $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$), \texttt{mean\_squared\_error\_gd} (full-batch gradient descent), and \texttt{mean\_squared\_error\_sgd} (stochastic gradient descent with mini-batch size $B=1$).
    \item \textbf{Probabilistic classification models (log-loss):} \texttt{logistic\_regression} and \texttt{reg\_logistic\_regression} trained via full-batch gradient descent on $y \in \{0, 1\}$.
\end{itemize}

Considering the binary nature of MICHD diagnosis, we selected $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression (with standard logistic regression as the $\lambda = 0$ special case) as our primary model. Logistic regression maps a feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D^*}$ to the posterior probability of a positive diagnosis via the sigmoid function $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$:
\begin{equation}
    p(y_i = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i}}.
\end{equation}
The regularized negative log-likelihood objective minimized during training is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Big[ y_i \log\sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i) + (1 - y_i) \log\big(1 - \sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i)\big) \Big] + \lambda \|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \geq 0$ penalizes large weights to prevent overfitting and mitigate collinearity. In accordance with project specifications, our functions in \texttt{implementations.py} return the unpenalized loss alongside the final weight vector $\mathbf{w}$.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
Our preprocessing pipeline in \texttt{run.py} transforms the raw $321$-dimensional inputs into a clean, well-conditioned design matrix of $D^* = 48$ features ($47$ variables plus an intercept):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Domain Feature Selection:} Using the BRFSS 2015 codebook and clinical literature~\cite{noauthor_www2statdukeeducr173sta102_sp16projcodebookhtml_nodate,cdc_brfss_codebook_2015}, we curated $47$ variables (\texttt{cvd\_variables}) spanning general health (\texttt{GENHLTH}, \texttt{PHYSHLTH}), cardiovascular history (\texttt{BPHIGH4}, \texttt{TOLDHI2}, \texttt{CVDSTRK3}, \texttt{HAREHAB1}), diabetes (\texttt{DIABETE3}), demographics (\texttt{SEX}, \texttt{\_AGE80}, \texttt{EDUCA}, \texttt{INCOME2}), and lifestyle risk factors (smoking, alcohol, exercise, and BMI).
    \item \textbf{Survey Code Recoding:} In \texttt{preprocess\_cols}, variable-specific codes representing ``None'' or zero days/drinks ($88, 888$) are mapped to $0$, whereas ``Don't know,'' ``Refused,'' or missing codes ($7, 9, 77, 98, 99, 777, 999, 7777, 9999$, and category codes $3, 14$) are replaced with \texttt{NaN}.
    \item \textbf{Imputation and Standardization:} In \texttt{preprocessing\_X}, any all-\texttt{NaN} or zero-variance columns are dropped, and remaining \texttt{NaN} entries are imputed using the column-wise median of the training split. Each feature is then standardized to zero mean and unit variance using the training set statistics ($\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{train}}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\text{train}}$) to ensure well-conditioned gradients, and a bias column of ones is prepended. Target labels $y \in \{-1, 1\}$ are mapped to $\{0, 1\}$ via \texttt{preprocessing\_Y}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning, Optimization, and Prediction}
We optimized $\mathbf{w}$ over $1{,}000$ full-batch gradient descent iterations:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{w}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{w}^{(t)} - \gamma \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(\sigma(\mathbf{w}^{(t)T} \mathbf{x}_i) - y_i\big) \mathbf{x}_i + 2\lambda \mathbf{w}^{(t)} \right).
\end{equation}
Given a trained weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ and decision threshold $\tau \in (0, 1)$, binary predictions are generated as $\hat{y}_i = 1$ if $\sigma(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i) \geq \tau$ and $0$ (or $-1$ for submission) otherwise.

To tune $(\lambda, \gamma, \tau)$, we performed a 5-fold cross-validation grid search (\texttt{SEED = 67}) maximizing the mean F1 score:
\begin{equation}
    \text{F1} = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}, \quad \text{Precision} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FP}}, \quad \text{Recall} = \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}}.
\end{equation}
Crucially, median imputation and standardization in \texttt{preprocessing\_X} were recomputed independently inside each cross-validation fold to prevent data leakage. Testing $\lambda \in \{0\} \cup [10^{-6}, 10^0]$ (20 log-spaced points), $\gamma \in [10^{-3}, 10^0]$ (20 log-spaced points), and $\tau \in [0.1, 0.7]$ (step $0.1$) yielded optimal hyperparameters $\lambda = 10^{-6}$, $\gamma = 0.775$, and $\tau = 0.20$.

Several clear patterns emerge from Table~\ref{tab:baselines}. First, unregularized \texttt{least\_squares} fails with a singular matrix error because derived BRFSS variables (e.g., \texttt{\_AGEG5YR}, \texttt{\_AGE65YR}, \texttt{\_AGE80}, and \texttt{\_AGE\_G}) are collinear, making $\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}$ rank-deficient. Adding a small $\ell_2$ penalty in \texttt{ridge\_regression} ($\lambda = 10^{-6}$) restores invertibility and matches full-batch \texttt{mean\_squared\_error\_gd} ($\text{F1} = 0.4109$ at $\tau = 0.20$). Second, \texttt{mean\_squared\_error\_sgd} with batch size $1$ over $1{,}000$ steps sees only $1{,}000$ samples ($0.38\%$ of each training fold) and suffers from high gradient variance ($\text{F1} = 0.3106 \pm 0.0852$). Finally, \texttt{reg\_logistic\_regression} and \texttt{logistic\_regression} at $\tau = 0.20$ outperform all linear MSE baselines by $+0.90$ F1 percentage points ($0.4199$ vs.\ $0.4109$) and $+1.39\%$ accuracy ($87.15\%$ vs.\ $85.76\%$), confirming that the log-loss objective and bounded sigmoid output better model binary disease probabilities.

Starting from a naive logistic regression baseline on all $315$ non-constant features with $\tau = 0.50$ (Configuration 1, $\text{F1} = 0.1814$), restricting the input space to our $47$ domain-selected features (Configuration 2) reduces the feature dimension by $85\%$ and speeds up gradient descent by $6.5\times$ while retaining almost identical performance. Applying BRFSS survey code cleaning (Configuration 3) increases the F1 score at $\tau=0.50$ from $0.1765$ to $0.2185$ ($+4.20$ percentage points), demonstrating the importance of replacing sentinel codes ($88, 77, 99$, etc.) before median imputation. The single largest gain comes from lowering the classification threshold from $\tau = 0.50$ to $\tau = 0.20$ (Configuration 3 $\to$ 6), which boosts the F1 score by $+20.14$ percentage points ($0.2185 \to 0.4199$). Combining feature selection, survey code recoding, $\tau = 0.20$, and $\ell_2$ regularization (Configuration 6) outperforms even the 315-feature model at $\tau = 0.20$ ($0.4199$ vs.\ $0.4149$) while achieving higher accuracy ($87.15\%$ vs.\ $86.59\%$) and lower model complexity.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Class Imbalance and the Precision--Recall Trade-off}
Because only $8.83\%$ of individuals in the training set have MICHD, a trivial classifier that predicts $\hat{y} = -1$ for every patient achieves $91.17\%$ accuracy but an F1 score of $0$. At the default threshold $\tau = 0.50$, regularized logistic regression achieves $91.46\%$ accuracy and $56.83\%$ precision, but detects only $13.53\%$ of true MICHD cases ($\text{F1} = 0.2185$). Lowering the decision threshold to $\tau = 0.20$ aligns the decision rule with the low prior probability of the positive class: recall nearly quadruples to $52.69\%$ while precision remains at $34.91\%$, nearly doubling the F1 score to $0.4199$. From a medical screening perspective, this trade-off is highly desirable, as false negatives (undiagnosed coronary heart disease) carry far more severe clinical consequences than false positives referred for follow-up evaluation.

\subsection{Model Interpretability and Clinical Validity}
Inspecting the learned weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ on the standardized full training set (final unpenalized log-loss $0.2231$) confirms that our model captures medically meaningful relationships. Aside from the negative intercept ($w_0 = -3.254$, reflecting the $8.83\%$ base rate), the largest positive predictors of MICHD are self-reported poor general health (\texttt{GENHLTH}, $w = +0.455$, coded $1=\text{Excellent}$ to $5=\text{Poor}$), advanced age (\texttt{\_AGE80}, $w = +0.442$; \texttt{\_AGEG5YR}, $w = +0.405$), high blood pressure (\texttt{\_RFHYPE5}, $w = +0.142$), and high cholesterol (\texttt{\_RFCHOL}, $w = +0.137$). Conversely, \texttt{SEX} ($w = -0.359$, coded $1=\text{Male}, 2=\text{Female}$), prior heart attack rehabilitation (\texttt{HAREHAB1}, $w = -0.262$, coded $1=\text{Yes}, 2=\text{No}$), and history of stroke (\texttt{CVDSTRK3}, $w = -0.184$, coded $1=\text{Yes}, 2=\text{No}$) have negative weights because higher numerical codes correspond to female sex or absence of prior cardiovascular events. All top-weighted features agree with established cardiological risk factors.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}
While our pipeline is fast, interpretable, and reproducible, several limitations remain. First, nominal and ordinal categorical variables (such as employment status \texttt{EMPLOY1}) were standardized directly without one-hot encoding, imposing an artificial linear ordering across categories. Second, retaining multiple collinear age and BMI representations splits weight magnitude across redundant predictors and caused unregularized \texttt{least\_squares} to fail; pruning collinear derived features or applying $\ell_1$ (Lasso) regularization would yield a sparser representation. Finally, because our model is strictly linear in the $47$ features, future work could incorporate polynomial interaction terms (e.g., age $\times$ smoking or BMI $\times$ hypertension) and inverse class-frequency weighting in the logistic loss to further improve recall and F1 performance.
